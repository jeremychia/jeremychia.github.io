The Singapore Budget for 2019 was tabled in parliament by Minister for Finance Heng Swee Keat on 18 February 2019. This year's budget is themed "Building a Strong, United Singapore" and is said to reflect Minister Heng's shift away from big government to a collaborative approach in governing Singapore - Singapore's recent budgets have given businesses a hand in positioning its economy for the future.

Notably, the key budget policies of Minister Heng include the [**Industry Transformation Maps**](https://www.mti.gov.sg/ITMs/Overview) (ITMs) in Budget 2016 and the [**International Partnership Fund**](https://www.straitstimes.com/singapore/singapore-budget-2017-new-partnership-fund-to-help-singapore-based-firms-scale-up-globally) in Budget 2017. This year's budget is poised to continue such efforts by helping businesses on their digital transformation journey and to scale-up globally, fueling Singapore's ambition to position its economy for the times ahead.

I thought it would be interesting to perform text mining on the Singapore Budget from 2009 to 2019 to observe trends in the choice of words used in the budget speech. I have utilised R to generate Wordclouds and perform a sentiment analysis of the budget speech, and will be sharing my observations below.

## Wordcloud of Singapore's Budget from 2009 to 2019

Using R, I managed to generate several wordclouds of the Singapore Budget speech from 2009 to 2019. These wordclouds are generated after removing common English stop words and other words such as 'will', 'year', 'also' etc. to optimise the wordcloud visualisation.

Here are some attention-grabbing words that pop up:

- '**Productivity**' took the center-stage in Budget 2010 - this coincided with the launch of the Productivity and Innovation Credit (PIC) scheme. Productivity continued to remain a *hot* word through to Budget 2013.
- '**Support**' seemed to be a key word in the Budget speeches from 2015 to 2019. The choice of word is in some way reflective of the attitude of governance, as I've mentioned earlier.

## Frequency of Words

R generates a frequency table of the words used to generate the wordcloud. I've enclosed a snapshot of the most frequent words in the budget speech. Interestingly, the word '**will**' appears significantly more frequently than all other words. In addition, the use of the word '**support**' peaked in Budget 2016, which was Minister Heng's first budget speech.

Here are some other fun facts:

- On average, the Singapore Budget speech has approximately 7,500 words. The longest budget speech was delivered in 2015 by then Minister for Finance Tharman Shanmugaratnam at approximately 9,100 words. The shortest budget speech was delivered in 2017 by Minister for Finance Heng Swee Keat, at approximately 5,900 words, after his recovery from a stroke in 2016.
- Across all 10 years, there are approximately 6,900 unique words used. Each budget uses on average 2,000 unique words.
- The reference to '**provide**', '**support**', '**singaporeans**', '**workers**' and '**families**' appeared to spike in 2011 and 2015 - election years. There is a similar trend this year as well.
- '**Technology**'-related terms have been used more frequently with each year, as with the word '**transformation**', with the spike of usage starting in 2016, during Minister Heng's launch of the ITMs.

## Sentiment Analysis

R's sentiment analysis algorithm assigns a score to each sentence in the budget speech, where +1 represents an entirely positive sentence and -1 represents an entirely negative sentence. Typically, the sentiment GI score assigned to sentences in the budget speech range between -1 and +1. For example, "boosting skills and productivity" attracts a score of +1, whereas "global crisis and recession" attracts a score of -1.

For this sentiment analysis, I used the dictionary with opinionated words from the Harvard-IV dictionary as used in the General Inquirer software. While it is not perfect in assigning scores, we will be able to gain a rough sense as to how positive, neutral or negative the sentences in the budget speech are.

Here are some observations:

- The most number of negative sentences appeared in 2009's budget speech. This is consistent with our understanding that the last recession happened in 2009.
- The positive spikes (representing "good" sentences) tend to be spread evenly throughout the budget speech. There is also a tendency for most of the positive sentences to be clustered at the start and end of the speech, whereas the more neutral component takes a bulk of the second half of the speech. Managing expectations is important, and it appears that mood-management techniques are employed in the budget speech.
