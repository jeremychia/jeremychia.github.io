*What can we learn from our Parliamentarian's first speeches?*

The 2020 Singapore General Elections ("GE2020"), held on 10 July 2020 amid the COVID-19 pandemic, was hailed as a significant development in Singapore's politics. Here's what we will remember GE2020 for:

1. **Establishment of the Office of the Leader of the Opposition**: The Worker's Party (WP) [won its second Group Representative Constituency](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2020/jul/11/singapores-ruling-party-dominates-election-but-opposition-makes-historic-gains) - Sengkang, bringing the number of WP's elected members to a post-independence high of 10. Joined by two Non-Constituency Members of Parliament (NCMP) from the Progress Singapore Party, the 12 members form Singapore's largest opposition parliamentary presence to-date. This development prompted the Prime Minister to [designate](https://vulcanpost.com/705046/leader-of-the-opposition-singapore/) MP Pritam Singh, the WP's leader, as Singapore's first *de jure* Leader of the Opposition.
2. **A "Crisis" Elections Anomaly**: In times of crisis, people often [favour a safe and proven government](https://lkyspp.nus.edu.sg/gia/article/ge2020-the-pros-and-cons-of-a-crisis-election). This election was potentially called in hopes of capitalising on the considerable praise of the Singapore government's response to COVID-19 - strong support measures, being lauded as the "[gold standard of contact tracing](https://www.todayonline.com/singapore/spores-gold-standard-covid-19-detection-far-more-effective-picking-cases-rest-world)", etc. This is, in spite of COVID-19 exposing a regrettable '[pandemic of inequality](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-54082861)' in Singapore. Despite the precedence of better performance post-crisis (PAP won 75.3% of the popular vote in 2001, post 9-11), the PAP's popular vote share dipped to one of the lowest since independence (2020: 61.2%). The result potentially signals that younger Singaporeans want a Singapore that *"enjoys tangible success and yet still embrace(s) intangible ideals"*.
3. **The "Social Media" Election (and POFMA)**: Owed to prevailing social distancing measures, election campaigns were fought [primarily on social media](https://coconuts.co/singapore/news/sg-elections-gauging-the-2020-election-by-internet-points/). This was also the first election post-legislation of the Protection from Online Falsehoods and Manipulation Act (POFMA), which empowers the executive branch of government to order the amendment of online posts that could harm the public interest; "fake news". POFMA was contentious for its [potential to stifle free speech](https://thediplomat.com/2020/07/singapores-first-election-under-the-fake-news-law/) in Singapore. Even so, there was noticeably more political discourse on my social media feeds this election season. Commentaries have also attributed the opposition parties' electoral successes to its [stellar social media initiatives](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/news/commentary/ge2020-singapore-workers-party-won-big-pritam-singh-leader-wp-12928596).

After the elections, winner candidates are elected to Parliament. This is when a new session of Parliament begins. This starts with the [President's Address](https://sprs.parl.gov.sg/search/sprs3topic?reportid=president-address-1420), which sets out the priorities for this term of Parliament. Thereafter, Members of Parliament (MP) will have five days allotted for a Debate on the President's Address.

## Debates on the President's Address

This debate is a [parliamentary tradition](https://www.parliament.gov.sg/newsroom/speaker's-blog/details/from-the-chair-a-parliament-for-extraordinary-times), officially on the Motion of Thanks to the President for his or her speech. Typically, the debate is when members discuss the government's plans. The speech is significant for [several reasons](https://www.straitstimes.com/politics/debate-on-the-presidents-address-4-things-you-need-to-know):

- **Members Outline their Parliamentary Ambitions**: This is the first opportunity for new MPs to make a speech in the House. In their speech, members speak on **what issues matter** to them most. There are 30 new MPs in the 14th Parliament; lots to look forward to in enhancing the diversity of perspectives in Singapore's Parliament with the different convictions that will come into play.
- **Setting the Tone for Future Sittings**: Issues that attract considerable attention from MPs will most likely be keenly discussed in further parliamentary sittings. The debates are reflective of the times and parliament's agenda.

Of the 93 members returned to the 14th Parliament, **74** made speeches during this debate. Those who did not give speeches were largely cabinet members or seasoned politicians. Some summary statistics:

- On average, most MP's speeches were **2,120** words long, delivered over **15** minutes, whereas cabinet members spoke for twice the time at **3,500** words over **30** minutes.
- The longest speech (and slowest) speech delivered was by [PM Lee Hsien Loong](https://sprs.parl.gov.sg/search/sprs3topic?reportid=president-address-1461), at approximately **5,650** words over **84** minutes; averaging **95** words per minute (wpm). In contrast, the shortest speech was delivered by MP [Cheng Li Hui](https://sprs.parl.gov.sg/search/sprs3topic?reportid=president-address-1470), at approximately **1,180** words over **8** minutes.
- The fastest speakers were MP [Louis Ng](https://sprs.parl.gov.sg/search/sprs3topic?reportid=president-address-1459) (**182 wpm**), MP [Tin Pei Ling](https://sprs.parl.gov.sg/search/sprs3topic?reportid=president-address-1452) (**170 wpm**), and MP [Murali Pillai](https://sprs.parl.gov.sg/search/sprs3topic?reportid=president-address-1459) (**167 wpm**). All very passionate MPs.
- For the *statistics geeks* - you'll be amused to know that the distribution of speaking speed is *somewhat* normally distributed with a mean of **136.8 wpm** and standard deviation of **17.1 wpm**. Our MPs, on average, speak faster than the regular person (at 100-130 wpm).
- There is a weak, but negative, linear relationship between our MPs' age and how fast they speak.

## Which topics featured most in 2020's debates?

The most commonly mentioned topics were: ***COVID-19***, the ***workforce,*** ***inequality, and education***. Other topics that were mentioned - *infrastructure*, *housing*, *mental health,* and *climate change*.

### COVID-19

Key Terms: *"COVID-19 Pandemic", "Circuit Breaker", "Global Financial Crisis", "Emerge Stronger", "Essential Services"*

- **88**% of all MPs used the word "COVID" in their speeches. This is undoubtedly the most pervasive issue globally. In their speeches, members pay tribute to the essential workers, talk about the struggles of the "Circuit Breaker", and give a little comfort that we will *emerge stronger*.
- Older politicians (those born before 1959) seemed to refer to "COVID" a lot more in their speech. The word "COVID" is **0.63%** of all words in these Members' speeches, vis-a-vis, on average, 0.35% for other members.
- Most speeches referred to the past impact of COVID-19 but did not mention substantially how the government should deal with future pandemics. This could signal that, in the minds of our politicians, Singapore society is moving beyond the COVID-19 pandemic.

### The Workforce

Key Terms: *"Hiring Practices", "EP Holders", "Salary Requirements", "Fair Consideration Framework", "Foreign/Low Wage/Migrant Workers", "Labour Movement", "Singaporean Core / Job Market", "Mid-Career", "Local Workforce"*

- **72%** of all MPs used terms referencing the job market and the workforce. This is expected, given that COVID-19 has severely affected Singapore's economy and that [retrenchments](https://www.straitstimes.com/singapore/manpower/retrenchments-in-singapore-more-than-double-to-8130-in-q2-mom) are on the rise. The word 'job(s)' was mentioned **638** times, whereas 'support' was mentioned **441** times.
- Half (**52%**) of the MPs who spoke deliberately referred "local" people / the Singaporean Core. This appears to be consistent in the government's recent initiative in [raising the salary criteria](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/news/singapore/employment-pass-s-pass-salary-criteria-foreign-workforce-mom-13055188) for Employment Pass (EP) holders, a measure seen as protecting the local workforce.
- Only **38%** of MPs spoke about migrant workers. Singapore's migrant worker community was very [badly affected](https://www.ricemedia.co/current-affairs-opinion-covid-19-migrant-worker-dorms-delay-inexcusable/) by the COVID-19 outbreaks. I hope more can be done for the migrant workers in our midst.

### Inequality and Education

Key Terms: *"Social Mobility", "Social Safety Nets", "Social Service", "Lower Income", "Inclusive Society", "Education System", "Primary School", "Pre-School"*

- **64%** of all members mentioned terms that pertain to *inclusiveness* and *social mobility*.
- Of terms relating to inequality, **"Safety Nets"** is the only term that is mentioned across all three parties. The devil is in the details. There is a concurrence that social safety nets should be strengthened; the key question is ***how***.
- "**Education**" featured more in speeches made by those born between **1965 to 1979**.
- "**Young**", not surprisingly, featured in the greatest proportion (0.61%, versus an average of 0.16%). Young voices are increasingly important, and they want to be heard.

## Observations on First-Time MPs at the Debates

Given that Singapore's 14th Parliament sees 30 first-term MPs, I decided to take a closer look at their speeches:

- The phrases "***we need to***" (0.16% versus an average of 0.08%) and "***I would like to***" (0.95% versus an average of 0.52%) feature in the largest proportion amongst first-term politicians.
- Other prominently featured topics that were not discussed earlier were raised by first-term MPs. First-time MPs talked a lot more about "**mental health**" (**38 times**, of total 45), "**climate change**" (**13 times**, of total 30). We will see new champions of these issues in our new MPs. This is a positive development.

## What does the MP's choice of words tell us?

The topics MPs choose to champion are often those close to their heart. As precedence has it, the topics unveiled in the President's Address debates will likely be what we can expect later debates in this parliamentary session to be about - a moving on from ***COVID-19***, securing jobs in our ***workforce***, and addressing the ***inequality*** in our society.

With the recent announcement of [in-principle approval](https://www.straitstimes.com/politics/in-principle-agreement-to-live-stream-parliament-sittings) to live-stream parliamentary debates, this will increase transparency, accessibility, and accountability to our parliamentarians and their speeches. I hope this increased accessibility will bring about an increase in the political participation of the Singaporean electorate. Collectively, through robust discourse, we can work towards a better Singapore for all.
