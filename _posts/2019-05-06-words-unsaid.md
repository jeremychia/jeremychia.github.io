In our first semester, we had a class [1] which required us to search and write a commentary on a selected article. If you were "lucky", the Teaching Assistant would select you to present your article in next week's class. Many of us would remember this endearingly as 'TechTalk'.

For our first 'TechTalk' submission, I selected an article on Metcalfe's Law and network effects with respect to Bitcoin. Much to my amusement, I was selected to share this with the class – despite having little idea on what the article was *trying* to say. I had to speak for three minutes about something I knew close to nothing about. Indeed, *smoking* [2] is an art.

Perhaps the first 'TechTalk' incident was a pre-cursor to what would follow in the rest of my days as a undergraduate. Somehow, I spend a lot of time questioning if I know what I am doing. Many of us do this too. In some sense, I guess not; the more you know, the less you *really* do.

Everything is better with the benefit of hindsight.

Before life gets busy once more, this time without a visible end, I thought it would be good to pen down some lessons from the last three years for anyone who is interested to have a read.

## 1. Our Most Important Attribute

*Why would any employer choose to hire you over all the others?*

Critical thinking is a skill that fortunately many of us came to develop over the course of our undergraduate careers. That being said, during our Business Law classes with A/P Harry Tan, our ability to think critically was certainly less than desirable.

I vividly remember A/P Tan beginning the semester with this question – "What is the most important attribute employers look for in their potential hires?". Many classmates were eager to chime in for an easy shot at additional class participation marks. They suggested – diligence, passion, integrity, etc. To which, A/P Tan reflected that those were *standard answers*. He left the question hanging for the next two-and-a-half hours as he went on with class.

"What you can do for them," he said roundly. "The interviewer looks for how much you can contribute to their company. Your utility. That's your most important attribute." [3]

I admit that was a fresh perspective. Good traits are important, but what you can accomplish is essential. With that in mind, we would spend the next three years *building* that value proposition.

## 2. Everything Happens for a Reason

*"It is fate that brought us together."*

Those were the words of Mrs. Chan Ai Lin, our Accounting 1 *Prof*, at the end of a hearty thirteen weeks of classes together. She used that phrase by way of saying that she did not choose us as her students and we did not choose her as our instructor, because our class indices in Year 1 were pre-assigned. This was largely true, given that it was near-impossible to swap classes in our first semester.

I find myself constantly appreciative of the fact that I met some of my closest university friends in that very Accounting I class. It was fate that brought us together – we were open to having a new group of friends, and we made the time to meet, keep in touch and register for the same classes over the next couple of semesters. The same can be said of everyone I have had the privilege of meeting over the last few semesters.

My opinion remains that fate, however strong, must still be supported by intentionality. Many of the friendships that have lasted are a result of sustained mutual effort, without which no amount of fate would have made up for the lack of effort. It is beneficial to be *intentional*.

## 3. Network Effects

*A network's effectiveness is determined by its nodes.*

Aside from the useful lesson on *smoking* in my first semester, there are two residual thoughts I had:

1. I should have taken my advice on the growing effectiveness of Bitcoin and invested in it back in 2016. If I did, I may not have been able to write this article as an undergraduate because I might have made enough money to quit school. I am kidding.
2. The effect of a network is positively related to the number of nodes in its network. This sums up Business School in a nutshell.

Our much-revered Financial Reporting Standards change from time to time; what we learn in business school is not immune to being rendered obsolete. However, connections established, and impressions formed are more likely than not to last a lifetime.

I believe the utility of business school is a propagation of the network effect – a community for you to enter the workforce with, and a group of people you can count on. This is especially important in a business environment where relationships ("关系") are valued. Business school is a perfect platform to know more people on a genuine level before entering the competitive world out there.

Build bridges, not burn them. There is little value in sacrificing a long-term relationship for something temporal and largely inconsequential in the long-run.

## 4. Permanent Interests

*Everyone will defend their interests.*

Lord Palmerston, a 19th-century British statesman, aptly describes: "nations have no permanent friends or allies, they only have permanent interests.". I find that this is true not only in international relations but to some extent in our personal lives too.

Whatever our interests may be, our duty is to follow those interests. I am convinced that every single organisation or person fights to defend their interests.

For example, the school has an interest to defend its reputation, and will employ a variety of tools using organisational architecture, soft, and hard control tactics to meet their interests. Most of us are familiar with the hard-line stance the career office takes against the renege of acceptance. Ultimately, while this policy reduces the likelihood of upsetting corporates, it ultimately serves the interest of protecting the school's reputation. As for us, students, the onus is on us to decisively turn down what may perhaps be a better opportunity to "protect' the school's interests. We owe it to ourselves to fight for ourselves – the truth is that no one will have your interests at heart as much as you do.

Many of us would have also encountered people who are transactional – i.e. they approach you when they need something from you and ignore you when they do not. Again – network effects; these people are connections to acknowledge but will, sadly, remain solely functional. Know the difference.

Nevertheless, be kind, be helpful. For those of us who would go the extra mile for someone without an agenda, remember to guard yourself – not everyone would the same for you. Also, not everyone values your advice as much as you value them [4].

Always remember to look out for yourself and be thankful for the few in your life who look out for you.

## 5. Living a *Life*

*Never get so busy making a living that you forget to make a life.*

Looking back, I remember the memories I made over the last three years more vividly than what was covered over the seminar classes. Who would forget playing *Mahjong* overnight, going to Bedok 85 from the far-west for a sumptuous bowl of *Bak Chor Mee* or doing 400 jumping-jacks in one night?

Being a part of university life only makes the entire experience more whole, more complete. Once again, I am grateful for the people who have been there to share and create these memories with.

On group work, some friends and I recently shared this sentiment in retrospect – between (i) a group that has a lot of fun but does not perform so well and (ii) a group that does well but has minimal interaction, we would rather have the former. I remember more fondly the groups I had a lot of fun slogging the project with, more so than the project teams that produced "good" grades.

Friendships are born out of adversity. It would be nice to avail yourself to some fun once every now and then, even if it meant having a lower grade. Worth it.

## Concluding Thoughts

University life is what we decide to make out of it. Though it is impossible to be entirely certain of what is coming next, I have grown to believe everything happens for a reason; miraculously, life falls into place in ways you least expect it to.

*Everything is better with the benefit of hindsight*.

Hopefully, these lessons (along with the several others which are not in this piece) will follow me through the next chapter in life.

Thank you, NTU, and the people who have made the school and this journey rich and fulfilling, and I do hope to keep in touch!
